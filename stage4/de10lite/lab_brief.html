Part A · How it works

A1. One chip, three homes

The RVBL-2 chip is the same design everywhere: the RISC-V core, its 8 KB data RAM, the ROM holding the LaneX firmware, one GPIO block with 8 pins, and one UART. What changes from home to home is what is wired to its edge. On the ASIC, its pins are physical pads. On AWS, the organisers' wrapper turns them into registers that ./emu pokes over PCIe. On the DE10-Lite, a ring of FPGA logic we wrote plays the outside world.

The chip in the middle is generated from the same rtl/ files and the same firmware.hex each time. The DE10-Lite build changes only two things, both value-for-value: the GPIO tri-state is replaced by a multiplexer (an FPGA has no tri-state inside), and the data RAM is written the way Quartus turns it into memory blocks.

Why LEDR4 stays on after a charged car

LEDR4–7 are wired straight to the chip's output pins P4–P7: they are the same lamps ./emu -o read on AWS. The protocol defines P4 as “the last vehicle was charged”, so it stays lit until the next vehicle is decided. The outside LEDs are different: they follow the same pins only for 4 s after each decision, so the booth looks calm between cars.

A3. Inside the FPGA

The chip sits in the middle. Everything around it is test equipment, the board's own version of the organisers' emu. It decides nothing about a vehicle: LaneX runs only on the chip.

The one-channel rule is what makes the board verifiable. A pin change waits until no frame is on the line, then nothing happens for 1.3 ms. So the order in which the chip sees events is exactly the order the testbench logs, and the golden model can replay it and must produce the chip's exact bytes. It did, in every one of the simulated runs.

The chip's memories on the FPGA

The 2,793-word firmware ROM becomes logic: a big lookup table built from logic elements, read in the same clock cycle as on the ASIC. The 8 KB data RAM becomes four 2 KB memory blocks, one per byte lane. The whole design, chip and fabric, uses 30 % of the FPGA.

Why a “tag reader emulator”?

A real gantry has a UHF RFID reader. Here the switches SW5:SW3 choose which tag the next vehicle carries, and the fabric sends the chip the exact R frame a reader would send (with a live time stamp and CRC) the moment the vehicle arrives.

A4. One vehicle, step by step

A toy car pushed under the sensor with the tag set to CAr. Times are for the real board; the sensor measures every 62.5 ms.

Arrival needs two readings in a row (about 125 ms) so a single echo glitch never makes a vehicle; departure needs three clear readings, so the gap between a car's windows never splits it in two. A missed echo counts as neither.

Rule 1: no 5 V on an FPGA pin

MAX 10 pins tolerate 3.3 V. Header pin 11 (5 V) feeds only the HC-SR04's VCC. Its Echo output is 5 V, so it always goes through the divider. Never connect Echo straight to pin 3, not even for a second.

Rule 2: wire with the USB unplugged

Change wiring only with the board unpowered. Then plug in and look before touching anything.

Tools

Multimeter (DC volts and ohms), the wiring card (wiring_card.png on your phone), Chrome or Edge, Quartus 25.1std.

Header orientation. JP1 is the 2 × 20 header. Pin 1 is marked on the board (a square pad or a small triangle). With pin 1 at the top left, odd pins run down the left column and even pins down the right. Pins 1–10 are GPIO_0–9, pin 11 is 5 V, pin 12 is GND, pin 29 is 3.3 V and pin 30 is GND.

E3

Power check and the breadboard rail

10 min
  1. Board powered, nothing on the header. Multimeter on DC volts: black probe on pin 30, red on pin 29.3.3 V (3.2–3.4).
  2. Black on pin 12, red on pin 11.about 5 V. This proves you hold the header the right way round.
  3. Unplug the USB. Jumper header pin 30 (GND) to the breadboard's blue (−) rail.
E4

Wire the ultrasonic sensor and its divider

20 min
  1. USB unplugged. Put the 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ resistors on the breadboard so they share one row (the midpoint). The 2 kΩ's other end goes to the GND rail.
  2. Measure on ohms before connecting anything else: midpoint to rail = 2 kΩ; across the 1 kΩ = 1 kΩ.about 2.0 kΩ and 1.0 kΩ (two 1 kΩ in series read 2.0 kΩ too).
  3. Midpoint row → header pin 3. The 1 kΩ's free end → HC-SR04 Echo.
  4. HC-SR04 Trig → header pin 4 (direct: the FPGA's 3.3 V is enough to trigger it).
  5. HC-SR04 VCC → pin 11 (5 V). HC-SR04 GND → pin 12.
  6. Check every wire against the card once more, then plug the USB in.the board boots as usual. If no Id appears or the board resets, unplug at once and recheck pins 11 and 12.
E5

Mount the sensor, learn the floor, measure your toys

20 min
  1. Tape the sensor to the gantry 15–25 cm above the table, looking straight down at the lane, both “eyes” level.
  2. With the lane empty, press KEY0 (hold it a moment). The board learns the floor from its first readings. SW8 down (sensor mode), SW2:SW1 = 00.LEDR0 off.
  3. Hold your hand flat 5 cm under the sensor.LEDR0 on; take it away: off.
  4. Set the tag to nonE (SW5:SW3 = 000) so no fare is involved. Place the toy car under the sensor and hold it there.LEDR2 on and the display shows its height, for example h 4.0 (4.0 cm). Write it down.
  5. Same with the lorry.its height, and LEDR1 (tall) if it is 6.0 cm or more.
  6. If the car reads 6.0 or more, or the lorry less than 6.0: open lanex.qsf, set set_parameter -name TALL_MM to a value halfway between the two heights in mm (car 45 mm, lorry 80 mm → 62), and repeat E1 steps 1–2.

E7

Lamps and buzzer (optional)

20 min
  1. USB unplugged. Wire each LED: header pin → resistor → long leg; short leg → GND rail.
  2. Buzzer module: VCC → pin 29 (3.3 V), GND → rail, I/O (or IN) → pin 8.
  3. Plug in.every lamp on for the 1 s lamp test, with a short beep.

E8

The laptop dashboard

20 min
  1. Set the adapter's jumper to 3.3 V. USB unplugged from the board. Wire adapter TXD → pin 1, RXD → pin 2, GND → pin 12. Leave its VCC/5V pin unconnected.
  2. Plug the adapter into the laptop. Device Manager → Ports (COM & LPT).a new COM port (CP210x, CH340 or USB Serial). If it has a yellow mark, install the driver from 08_quartus_setup.md §8.
  3. Power the board. Open stage4\de10lite\lanex_live.html in Chrome or Edge → Connect board → choose that COM port.“Connected · 115,200 bps”.
  4. Click Fingerprint the ROM now.the board shows Id.56dE; the page shows 56DE in green, “Matches this build”.
  5. Push a car (tag CAr).a “Charged” card, RM 2.50, CRC ✓, then “Signed ✓ Chaskey-12”.
  6. Choose Taxi → Report stolen. Set the tag to CAb, wait 3 s, push a car.StOLEn on the board and a red card. Then Clear and push again after 3 s: charged RM 1.30.
  7. Close lane.board idle shows CLOSEd, LEDR7. Open lane to undo.
  8. Ask for statistics.a Statistics row with the vehicle count.

The watchdog you will probably never see

If the chip ever failed to answer within 1.5 s after a vehicle left, the board would restart it by itself (a warm boot: Id.56dE, or no Id if it still does not answer). It is tested in simulation by silencing the chip on purpose; the real chip has never needed it.
